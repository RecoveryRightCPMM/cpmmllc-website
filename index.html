Claims & Appeals

Why your 90837 keeps getting denied, and what to do about it

CPT 90837 is the 60-minute psychotherapy code, and it is one of the most audited codes in behavioral health. When it keeps coming back denied, the service is rarely the problem. The claim, the documentation, or the payer's rules usually are.

What 90837 requires

90837 is billed for psychotherapy of 53 minutes or more with the patient. A session of 38 to 52 minutes is 90834. If your note doesn't show the time, the payer has no way to confirm the longer code, and many will deny or down-code it.

The most common reasons it's denied

  • Time isn't documented. The note needs start and stop times or total minutes of psychotherapy.
  • Frequency flags. Some payers watch how often a provider bills 90837 and require prior authorization or records after a set number of sessions.
  • Medical necessity. The note doesn't explain why a longer session was clinically needed.
  • Missing authorization. Often shown as CARC 197 (precertification absent).
  • Telehealth errors. The wrong modifier or place of service for a virtual session.
  • Provider setup. The rendering provider isn't credentialed or linked to the group with that payer.

What to do when it's denied

  1. Read the denial and remark codes on the EOB. They tell you whether this is a fixable data error or a true denial.
  2. If it's a data error (wrong modifier, missing information), correct it and send a corrected claim.
  3. If it's a true denial, appeal with the session note, the documented time, and the payer's own policy language.
  4. Note the appeal deadline the day the denial arrives.

How to prevent the next one

Add a required time field to your note template, check each payer's policy on 90837, and watch how often each clinician bills it. A pattern of denials on one payer usually has one root cause.

Parity Law

Using MHPAEA parity law to win appeals you think you've lost

The Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) requires most health plans to cover mental health and substance use care on terms no more restrictive than medical and surgical care in the same category. Many behavioral health denials quietly break that rule, and most practices never raise it on appeal.

Where parity problems hide

Obvious differences, like a higher copay for therapy, are rare now. The problems are usually in "non-quantitative treatment limitations," the rules that limit care without a number attached:

  • Prior authorization required for therapy but not for comparable medical visits
  • Stricter medical necessity criteria or more frequent reviews
  • Session limits or concurrent review applied only to behavioral health
  • Reimbursement methods that pay behavioral health providers on different terms

The rules today

Since 2021, federal law has required plans to complete written comparative analyses showing their treatment limitations meet parity, and to provide them on request. A 2024 federal rule added more requirements, but in May 2025 the federal agencies paused enforcement of the new 2024 provisions while they reconsider them. The underlying law and the earlier rules still apply. Check the current status before relying on any specific provision.

How to use parity in an appeal

  1. Name the limitation that caused the denial, such as an authorization rule or a medical necessity standard.
  2. Request the plan documents and the plan's comparative analysis for that limitation. You may need the patient's authorization to act as their representative.
  3. Compare how the plan applies the same kind of rule to medical and surgical care.
  4. State the parity issue clearly in your Level 1 or Level 2 internal appeal.

Parity doesn't apply to every plan. Traditional Medicare, for example, is not subject to MHPAEA, and some small or government plans have exceptions.

Telehealth

Modifier 95 vs. modifier 93: getting telehealth billing right

Telehealth is now a normal part of behavioral health, but the billing rules still differ by payer and have changed many times since 2020. Most telehealth denials come from a mismatch between how the session happened and how it was coded.

The basics

  • Modifier 95: a live session with both audio and video.
  • Modifier 93: a live session by audio only (phone).
  • POS 10: telehealth where the patient is at home.
  • POS 02: telehealth where the patient is somewhere other than home.

Some commercial payers instead want the place of service you would use in person (such as POS 11) with modifier 95. Always follow the specific payer's telehealth policy.

Common mistakes

  • Billing modifier 95 when the video failed and the session finished by phone
  • A place of service that doesn't match where the patient was
  • Billing a code the payer doesn't cover by telehealth or by audio only
  • The clinician isn't licensed in the state where the patient was located

Document audio-only sessions carefully

For audio-only care, note where the patient was, why video wasn't used (for example, the patient couldn't use video or declined it), the patient's consent, and the time spent.

Build a payer grid

List each payer you bill with its required modifier, place of service, and audio-only rules. Review it whenever a payer updates its policy. Medicare's telehealth rules in particular have changed with each federal extension, so confirm the current rules before billing.

Medicare & Medicaid

Read the error before you rebill: fixing Medicare and Medicaid denials

Resubmitting a Medicare or Medicaid claim without understanding why it failed usually produces the same result, or a duplicate-claim denial. The fix starts with reading the error correctly.

Rejected or denied?

  • Rejected: the claim failed front-end checks and was never processed, for example an invalid NPI or a member ID that doesn't match. Fix the data and submit it as a new claim.
  • Denied: the claim was processed and not paid. Resending the same claim triggers a duplicate denial. Use a corrected claim (frequency code 7) or the payer's reopening or appeal process instead.

Read the codes

The group code tells you who is responsible: CO (the provider, not billable to the patient), PR (the patient), OA or PI (other or payer-initiated adjustments). The claim adjustment reason code (CARC) tells you why, and the remark code (RARC) adds the detail.

Common behavioral health causes

  • The rendering clinician isn't enrolled with Medicare or the state Medicaid program, or supervision rules weren't met
  • Taxonomy or provider type doesn't match the service billed
  • The claim went to state Medicaid when the patient is in a managed Medicaid plan, or the reverse
  • Coding-pair edits or frequency limits on same-day services
  • Timely filing: Medicare generally allows 12 months from the date of service; Medicaid deadlines vary by state and plan

Before you rebill

Confirm eligibility and enrollment, fix the specific error the codes point to, choose the right submission type, and log the date so you stay inside the filing limit.

Practice Management

A solo therapist's guide to handling payer pushback

When you are the clinician and the billing department, a denial can feel personal. It isn't. Most denials for solo practices fall into a few predictable types, and what you do in the first 30 days decides whether the money comes back.

Days 1–3: read, don't react

  • Separate patient responsibility (copay, deductible) from an actual denial.
  • Check the patient's eligibility and any authorization for the dates of service.
  • Write down the denial and remark codes.

Days 3–10: fix what's fixable

  • Correct data errors such as a wrong code, modifier, or member ID, and submit a corrected claim.
  • If you call the payer, record the date, the representative's name, and the call reference number.

Days 10–30: appeal what's wrong

  • If the denial is incorrect, file a Level 1 internal appeal with your note, the payer's policy, and a short, clear letter.
  • Most employer plans allow at least 180 days from the denial to appeal, but individual plans and provider contracts can set shorter limits. Don't wait.

Habits that protect you

  • Keep a simple denial log: patient initials, date of service, payer, code, deadline, status.
  • Never bill a patient for a CO (contractual) adjustment.
  • Don't write off a denial until you know why it happened.

Revenue Cycle

Is your practice leaving money on the table? A 5-minute billing checklist

You don't need a full audit to know whether you have a billing problem. Pull these five numbers from your billing system or clearinghouse reports.

1. Denial rate

Denied claims divided by claims submitted over the last 90 days. Many practices aim to keep this under about 5 to 10 percent.

2. A/R over 90 days

The share of your outstanding balance that is more than 90 days old. A common target is under about 15 to 20 percent. Old balances are the hardest to collect.

3. Clean claim rate

The share of claims paid on first submission without corrections. A low rate means errors are going out the door.

4. Charge lag

How many days pass between the session and the claim being sent. Delays shrink your time to fix problems before filing limits.

5. Unworked denials

How many denials have no action taken, and the date of the oldest appeal deadline among them.

How to read your results

If two or more numbers are off, you likely have a pattern, not bad luck. Benchmarks vary by practice type and payer mix, so use them as a starting point, not a verdict.

HIPAA

42 CFR Part 2 changes: updating your Notice of Privacy Practices

In February 2024, HHS finalized changes that bring the confidentiality rules for substance use disorder records (42 CFR Part 2) closer to HIPAA. The compliance date was February 16, 2026. Practices that haven't updated their policies and Notice of Privacy Practices should act now.

Who is affected

  • Federally assisted substance use disorder programs
  • Practices and organizations that receive Part 2 records, including through care coordination or integrated care

What changed, in brief

  • Patients can give a single consent for future uses of their records for treatment, payment, and health care operations.
  • Breach notification requirements now apply to Part 2 records.
  • Notices of Privacy Practices must reflect the new Part 2 rules.
  • Stronger protections remain against using records in legal proceedings against the patient without consent or a court order.

What to do

  1. Update your Notice of Privacy Practices and post the new version.
  2. Review your consent forms against the new single-consent option.
  3. Confirm how Part 2 records are labeled and shared in your systems.
  4. Train staff who handle records, billing, and release of information.
Serve SUD patients? Billing for these programs has its own consent and documentation traps. Ask a Specialist about your situation.

HIPAA

The HIPAA Security Rule update: what behavioral health practices should prepare for

In January 2025, HHS proposed the largest update to the HIPAA Security Rule since it was written. As of this writing it is still a proposal, and the timing of a final rule is uncertain. Much of what it proposes is already good practice, and preparing now costs little compared with a breach.

What the proposal would require

  • Multi-factor authentication for systems that hold patient information
  • Encryption of patient information at rest and in transit
  • A written inventory of technology assets and a map of how data moves
  • Vulnerability scans at least every six months and penetration testing at least once a year
  • The ability to restore critical systems and data within 72 hours
  • An end to "addressable" safeguards, making most requirements mandatory
  • Business associates confirming their safeguards in writing

Why it matters for behavioral health

Psychotherapy and substance use records are among the most sensitive data in health care. Small practices are frequent targets because they often lack basic protections like multi-factor authentication.

Steps you can take now

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, your EHR, and your billing system.
  2. Confirm your devices and cloud storage are encrypted.
  3. Update your security risk analysis.
  4. Make sure every vendor that touches patient data has a signed BAA.

HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices & Compliance Statement

Effective June 25, 2026 · Required by 45 CFR § 164.520

THIS NOTICE DESCRIBES HOW HEALTH INFORMATION ABOUT YOU MAY BE USED AND DISCLOSED AND HOW YOU CAN GET ACCESS TO THIS INFORMATION. PLEASE REVIEW IT CAREFULLY.

Part A — HIPAA compliance statement

Coastal Practice Medical Management is committed to compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA), as amended, including the Privacy Rule, Security Rule, and HITECH Act provisions.

Our role under HIPAA

CPMM functions as a Business Associate when providing services to covered entities. As a Business Associate, CPMM:

  • Uses and discloses PHI only as permitted by Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) and HIPAA regulations
  • Accesses only the minimum PHI necessary to perform contracted services
  • Coordinates services through your own billing systems rather than submitting claims from its own systems
  • Maintains reasonable administrative, physical, and technical safeguards
  • Reports any breach of PHI within 60 days of discovery per 45 CFR § 164.410
  • Requires a signed BAA with every covered entity before accessing any PHI
  • Does not retain PHI beyond the period needed to fulfill contracted services, consistent with BAA terms

Business Associate Agreement requirement

A signed BAA is required before any service involving PHI begins. The BAA is separate from the Services Agreement.

Security safeguards

  • Administrative: PHI handling policies, workforce HIPAA training, designated privacy contact
  • Physical: restricted access to locations where PHI may be stored; secure disposal
  • Technical: encrypted email and secure file transfer; password-protected access; regular access review

Website and forms

Our website forms are for business inquiries only. Do not submit PHI through any website form. PHI needed for an engagement is transmitted only through secure, agreed-upon channels.

Part B — Notice of privacy practices

How we may use and disclose health information

  • Healthcare operations: claims analysis, appeal preparation, and billing audits on behalf of your provider
  • Payment: appeal letters, payer communications, and payment reconciliation
  • As required by law: when required by federal or state law, regulation, or court order
  • Subcontractors: with subcontractors who assist CPMM, under appropriate BAAs

Your rights

  • Right to access your PHI
  • Right to request amendment
  • Right to an accounting of disclosures for the prior 6 years
  • Right to request restrictions
  • Right to request confidential communications
  • Right to a paper copy of this Notice

Breach notification

If a breach of unsecured PHI occurs, CPMM will notify the covered entity without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery.

Complaints

You may file a complaint with CPMM or with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights. You will not be retaliated against for filing a complaint.
